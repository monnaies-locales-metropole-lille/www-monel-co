# Cyclos Database — Data Exposure Risk Assessment

**Companion to** INC-2026-08-21-001 (`2026-08-21-spip-rce-webshell.md`)
**Date** 2026-08-26
**Scope** Risk to Monel / MLML members arising from potential disclosure of the Cyclos
production database (`cyclos4`)

---

## 1. Basis and confidence

This assessment is derived from direct analysis of the `2026-08-26-daily` `pg_dump` of
`cyclos4` — the same artefact that was exposed. Counts and field inventories below are
measured, not estimated.

**Exposure status: presumed, not proven.** No log evidence exists that the database was
read (PostgreSQL `log_connections` was disabled), and none can be recovered. Two facts
drive the presumption:

- On `prod-mlml-01`, a 41 MB world-readable dump sat in a directory the compromised web
  account could read, for the duration of the intrusion.
- On `preprod-mlml-01`, a synced copy of the same dump sat beside a **live, interactive
  file-manager webshell for three days** (23–26 August), on a host the attacker fully
  controlled.

**Integrity is separately confirmed intact** — all 417 tables were compared across the
intrusion window and nothing was modified (incident report §5.7). This assessment therefore
concerns **confidentiality only**. No member's balance, transaction or account was altered.

## 2. What the database contains

### 2.1 Identity and authentication

| Data | Volume |
|---|---|
| User accounts | 154 total — 128 active, 23 purged, 3 disabled (150 users, 4 operators) |
| Usernames | 154 |
| Email addresses | 131 |
| Password hashes | 194 (all bcrypt) |
| Login records with IP address | 1,042 |

### 2.2 Organisation profiles (122 organisations)

Business-register data — **all of which is already public record in France** via INSEE
Sirene and Infogreffe:

`SIRET` · `SIREN` · `RCS` · `TVA_Intra` · `raison_sociale` · `forme_juridique` ·
`code_naf_v4` · `Effectif` · `date_creation` · `site_internet` · `page_linkedin` ·
`Description_Organisation`

### 2.3 Named contact persons — the principal personal data

For each organisation, a designated individual:

`Nom_contact` · `Prenom_contact` · `Courriel_contact` · `telephone_contact`

Approximately **122 named individuals** with full name, direct email and direct telephone
number, each explicitly linked to a named employer.

Plus 116 postal addresses, 8 phone records, 20 contact-info records.

### 2.4 Embedded documents — monthly account statements

**773 files totalling 33.5 MB are embedded in the dump itself** (as PostgreSQL large
objects — they travel with the file; they are not merely referenced): 686 PDF, 51 JPEG,
36 PNG.

**658 of the 686 PDFs are monthly member account statements.** This was confirmed by
extracting and decoding a sample from the dump (generated by `openhtmltopdf`, one page
each, subset-font encoded).

| | |
|---|---|
| Statements | **658** |
| Members covered | **18** |
| Statements per member | median **44** (16 members have ≥12) |
| Period | **2022-12 → 2026-07**, 45 distinct months |

Each statement contains, for one member:

- `Raison Sociale`, `SIRET`, `Code NAF`, account number, `Type de compte`
- **`Solde`** (balance), **`Solde disponible`** (available balance),
  **`Limite du solde positif`** (credit limit)
- **An itemised transaction table: `Date` | `Depuis / vers` | `Montant`** — every
  transaction with its date, **named counterparty**, and amount

The remaining ~28 PDFs and the images are branding and marketing assets (logos, banners),
plus a small number of business documents: `kbis` (6), `facture` (8), `attestation` (4),
`bilan` (3).

### 2.5 Financial data — the tables are small, the statements are not

| Table | Rows |
|---|---|
| `accounts` | 18 |
| `account_balances` | 17 |
| `transfers` | 6 |
| `transactions` | 13 |

**These row counts are misleading and must not be read as the extent of financial
exposure.** The live tables hold almost no transaction history — but the 658 generated
statements retain **roughly four years of it** for the 18 members who hold accounts.

The practical consequence: an attacker holding this dump can reconstruct, for 18 named
organisations, their monthly balances and credit limits over four years, and **the full
graph of who traded with whom, when, and for how much** within the network.

## 3. What the database does NOT contain — verified

These were tested for across the entire 89 MB dump and returned **zero matches**:

| Data type | Result |
|---|---|
| IBAN / bank account numbers | **0** |
| BIC / SWIFT codes | **0** |
| French social security numbers (NIR) | **0** |
| Payment card numbers | **0** |
| Security questions / answers | **0 of 154 populated** |
| Plaintext or reversibly-encrypted passwords | **none — all 194 hashes are bcrypt** |

This substantially bounds the risk. There is no direct financial-fraud vector: an attacker
holding this database cannot initiate a payment, debit an account, or impersonate a member
to a bank.

## 4. Password exposure in detail

All 194 hashes are bcrypt, which is designed to be slow to attack. Detail matters:

| Cost factor | Count | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| `$2a$10$` | 192 | Sound. ~10⁴ hashes/sec/GPU — strong passwords remain infeasible to recover; weak or common ones are still crackable. |
| `$2a$04$` | **2** | **Weak.** Cost 4 is 64× faster to attack than cost 10. These two credentials should be treated as recoverable. |

The password policy for the main type (`avoid_obvious`, `avoid_repeated_value`, bcrypt
strength 10) is reasonable.

**95 of 128 active accounts hold only a `RESET`-status password** — an administrator-set
temporary credential the member never replaced with their own. Only 55 users have ever set
an `ACTIVE` password. Temporary credentials are frequently generated to a predictable
pattern; if that is the case here, those 95 hashes are materially weaker than the bcrypt
cost factor implies.

**The dominant password risk is not this system — it is reuse elsewhere.** Anyone who
recovers a password from this database will try it against the member's email and other
services.

## 5. Risk analysis by category

| Category | Likelihood | Severity | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Commercial confidentiality — 18 members** | **High** | **High** | Four years of balances, credit limits and counterparty-level transaction history. See §5.1. |
| **Targeted phishing / BEC** | **High** | **High** | Enabled across ~122 contacts, and made far more convincing for the 18 with statements. See §6. |
| Password reuse / credential stuffing | Moderate | High | bcrypt slows but does not prevent recovery of weak passwords; 2 hashes at cost 4; 95 unreplaced temporary credentials. |
| Named contact details | **High** | Moderate | Name, direct email, direct phone, tied to a named employer — the enabler for §6. |
| Business identity data | Low | Low | SIRET, SIREN, RCS, TVA, NAF, effectif are public record. Disclosure adds negligible harm. |
| Postal addresses | Low | Low–Moderate | Mostly business premises (public). Sole traders may be registered at a home address. |
| IP addresses | Low | Low | 1,042 login records. Personal data under GDPR, but limited practical harm. |
| Direct financial fraud | **Very Low** | — | No IBAN, BIC or card data present — no payment can be initiated from this data. |

### 5.1 Commercial confidentiality — the most concrete harm

For **18 named organisations**, the statements disclose:

- **Monthly balances and credit limits over ~4 years** — a proxy for the scale and
  trajectory of their activity in the network
- **The complete transaction graph** — the `Depuis / vers` column names the counterparty
  on every line

This is commercially sensitive in a way the identity data is not. It reveals **who trades
with whom, how often, and for how much** among a small, named group of local businesses.
Plausible consequences: competitive intelligence, inference about a business's health from
its balance trajectory, and pressure or embarrassment arising from disclosed trading
relationships.

Note the legal distinction: for organisations this is confidential business information
rather than GDPR personal data — but where a member is a sole trader or micro-entreprise,
the business and the individual are the same person, and it **is** personal data. That
distinction should be checked against the 18 members' legal forms (`forme_juridique` is in
the dataset).

## 6. The realistic harm scenario

Not mass fraud — **targeted social engineering**, and the database is unusually well suited
to it.

An attacker holds, for ~122 organisations simultaneously: the organisation's legal name and
registration numbers, its designated contact's full name, that person's direct email and
direct phone, and the verified fact that this organisation is a member of a named local
currency network.

That combination makes a convincing approach possible without any further research:

- Email or telephone contact referencing Monel/MLML membership by name, citing the correct
  organisation and the correct contact person.
- A "security incident — please reset your Cyclos password" message, which is **especially
  credible while a genuine incident is being communicated**. Whoever holds this data can
  send it before or alongside your own notification.
- Invoice or payment-detail fraud between members who can be shown to know one another.

**The 18 members with statements are materially more exposed than the rest.** An approach
to them can cite a real balance, a real past transaction, or a real trading counterparty —
detail that only a genuine insider would normally possess, and which defeats the usual
advice to "check whether the sender knows real details about your account." Invoice-
redirection fraud between two members known to trade with each other is the concrete
scenario.

**The remaining ~104 named contacts** are exposed to the same phishing risk at lower
credibility — name, organisation and membership, but no financial detail.

The timing risk is worth stating plainly: your own breach notification is the perfect cover
for a phishing campaign built from the leaked data. This shapes how you should communicate
(§8).

## 7. What remains undetermined

1. **Whether the database was in fact read.** Unknowable (§1).
2. **Contents of the remaining ~28 non-statement PDFs** — `facture`, `kbis`,
   `attestation`, `bilan`. Kbis extracts and filed accounts are largely public record in
   France, so the expected marginal harm is low, but they have not been individually
   reviewed.
3. **Legal form of the 18 members holding accounts** — determines whether their statement
   data is confidential business information or GDPR personal data (§5.1).
4. **Whether the `/var/www/mlml.fr` database was also exposed** — a planted Adminer client
   with PostgreSQL support was found there; the database it connects to has not yet been
   identified (incident report §8, item 6d).

> The previous version of this assessment recorded the PDF contents as the largest open
> question and rated transaction exposure "Very Low" on the basis of the table row counts.
> Both have been corrected above: the statements were confirmed to contain four years of
> counterparty-level transaction history that the live tables no longer hold.

## 8. Recommended actions

### Technical — before notification

1. **Force a password reset for all 128 active accounts.** Invalidate existing sessions.
   This neutralises the credential risk regardless of what was recovered.
2. **Prioritise the two `$2a$04$` credentials** — treat those as compromised outright.
3. **Review a sample of the embedded PDFs** to close §7.1 before finalising the member
   communication, since it may change what must be disclosed.
4. Complete the outstanding technical remediation in the incident report — in particular,
   preprod rebuild and the `mlml.fr` database identification.

### Member communication

Notify the ~122 named contacts and all active account holders. The communication should:

- State plainly what was exposed and what was **not** — specifically that **no bank
  details, card numbers or payment data were held in this system**, and that **no balance
  or transaction was altered**. Both are verified and both are genuinely reassuring.
- **Tell the 18 account-holding members separately and specifically.** Their exposure is
  different in kind: four years of statements including balances, credit limits and named
  counterparties. A generic notice would understate what happened to them, and they are the
  ones who most need to treat unexpected payment-related contact with suspicion — including
  messages that appear to come from another member they genuinely trade with.
- **Warn explicitly that the leaked data enables convincing phishing**, and state which
  channels you will and will not use. Commit to never asking for a password by email or
  telephone.
- Tell members a password reset has already been forced — so any message asking them to
  "click here to reset" is fraudulent.
- Advise anyone who reused their Cyclos password elsewhere to change it there too.
- Give a named human contact for verification, so recipients can check whether a message
  purporting to come from Monel is genuine.

### Regulatory

Under **GDPR Article 33**, notification to the CNIL is required within 72 hours of
awareness unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to rights and freedoms. Given
confirmed unauthorised access to hosts holding this data, and ~122 identified individuals
with directly contactable personal data, the "unlikely to result in a risk" threshold is
difficult to sustain.

Under **Article 34**, communication to data subjects is required where there is a **high**
risk. The verified absence of bank, card and national-identifier data argues against the
highest tier. Against that: four years of financial statements for 18 identified members,
and a strong targeted-phishing exposure across ~122 named individuals. Where any of the 18
is a sole trader or micro-entreprise, their statement data is personal data and the
high-risk threshold becomes considerably harder to argue against (§5.1).

Notifying is in any case the only way to give members the warning that actually protects
them.

**This is a legal determination for whoever holds data-protection responsibility at MLML,
not one this assessment can make.** The factual basis for it is §2, §3 and §6.
